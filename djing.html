DJing on the Prime GO+

Getting everything out of a standalone deck — and learning to actually DJ

The Denon DJ Prime GO+ is an unusual machine: a full two-deck standalone system with a 7-inch touchscreen, stem separation and a six-hour battery, that fits in a backpack and needs no laptop at all. That combination removes most of the excuses — but a deck that does everything for you still won't tell you when to do it. This is both halves: the workflow that makes the hardware disappear, and the craft that survives whatever you play on.

① What You're Holding

The Machine, Briefly

Worth knowing what you've actually got, because a lot of DJ advice online assumes a laptop and a controller, and that isn't this. The Prime GO+ runs Engine DJ on board — the analysis, the library, the effects and the recording all happen inside the unit.

Two decks rather than four is worth reframing as a feature. Four-deck DJing is mostly a studio-style exercise; almost all the greatest club sets ever played were two turntables. Two decks means every transition is a real decision, and it's much harder to hide behind layering.

② Where Sets Are Actually Won

Preparation

The gap between DJs is mostly preparation, and almost none of it happens while you're playing. Engine DJ Desktop is where you do it: analyse, fix, cue, tag, and render stems, then sync it all to a drive. Do this properly once and the unit becomes an instrument rather than a browser.

Analyse everything, then check it

Analysis gets BPM and key automatically, and it is right most of the time — which is exactly the problem. Half-time and double-time errors are common on anything with a sparse or syncopated intro. Scan the BPM column for outliers before a gig.

Beatgrids are the foundation

A wrong grid means the track drifts out mid-mix and there is nothing you can do live. Anything not made to a click — old funk, disco, live recordings — needs its grid checked and anchored by hand. This is the single highest-value prep task.

Cue points with a system

Four pads means four choices, so make them the same four every time: first downbeat, the drop, the breakdown, the outro. Consistency means your hands know where things are without reading the screen.

Render stems in advance

Stem separation renders on the desktop app by drag-and-drop, for a track, a playlist, or a whole crate. Do it before the gig, not during — and do it for the tracks you'd actually want an acapella or a drums-only version of, not everything.

Loop points where you'll need them

Save a loop on the intro and the outro of anything with a short mixable section. That turns an awkward eight-bar window into as much time as you want.

Two drives, always

Sync your library to a USB stick and an SD card with identical contents. Drives fail, and they fail at the worst moment. The SD slot means your backup lives inside the unit.

A useful discipline borrowed from studio work: prepare in a different session from playing. Preparing while you practise turns into browsing, and browsing is not practice.

③ The Real Skill

The Library Is the Instrument

Streaming puts a hundred million tracks on a 7-inch screen, which sounds like an advantage and is mostly a trap. You cannot select from a hundred million tracks in eight bars. Good DJs play from a small, deeply known collection and use the vast one for research.

HabitWhy
Playlists by function, not genre"Openers", "peak", "after the peak", "rescue" beats "House" — you never think I need a house track, you think I need to lift this
Know 100 tracks properlyWhere every breakdown is, how every intro behaves. That's worth more than owning 10,000
Prepare a gig-specific list60–100 tracks for the night, in a playlist. You'll play 25 of them and never be lost
Cache streaming for the setAnything you might play should be local. See the warning below
Tag by energy, 1–5The only metadata that helps in the moment. Key and BPM are already on screen; "how hard does this hit" isn't

The streaming warning, plainly: a venue's Wi-Fi will fail, and licences and catalogues change without notice. Streaming is superb for discovery and for the unplanned request; it is not a foundation to build a paid gig on. Anything you need should be on the drive, downloaded and analysed. The GO+ has two media slots for exactly this reason.

④ The Craft

Beatmatching, Phrasing and the Mix

Sync will beatmatch for you and there is no shame in using it. But sync solves the easy problem. The hard one, the one that separates a set that flows from one that lurches, is phrasing — and no machine does it for you.

Dance music is built in fours: four beats to a bar, four bars to a phrase, eight or sixteen bars to a section, and sections almost always change on a multiple of eight. A mix that starts on the wrong bar will feel wrong even if the beats line up perfectly, because the two tracks' phrases are fighting. Count bars, start your incoming track on a downbeat at the top of a phrase, and half of mixing is already solved.

EQ, not just the fader

Two kicks at once is mud. The basic move: as you bring the new track in, pull the outgoing bass down. Only one track should own the low end at any moment — the same rule the Mixing guide gives for a kick and a bassline.

Learn to beatmatch by ear anyway

Not for purity — because it teaches you to hear drift, which is what tells you a beatgrid is wrong before the mix falls apart. Practise with sync off for ten minutes a session.

Long blends aren't automatically better

A 64-bar blend is a skill; so is a hard cut on the one. Beginners default to the long blend because it feels safe, and a set of nothing but blends has no punctuation.

Use the loops

If the outgoing track's outro is too short, loop its last eight bars and take the time you need. This is the most under-used button on the unit and it turns panic into composure.

Bar-counting is a rhythm skill, not a DJ skill, and it trains faster away from the decks. The Rhythm & Beats guide's counting exercises are directly applicable — if you can hear where bar one is in anything, phrasing stops being work.

⑤ Restraint

Pads and Effects

The Prime GO+ has 26 main effects, two sweep knobs, echo on the fader and ten touch effects. You will use about five of them regularly, and that is not a failure — it's what good DJs do. Effects are punctuation. A set with an effect on every transition reads like a paragraph made entirely of exclamation marks.

ToolUse it forOverused when
Echo / delayCovering a cut, ending a phrase, throwing a vocal tailEvery transition. It stops meaning anything by the third one
Filter (sweep)The workhorse. Bringing a track in from nothing, building tensionRarely — but a filter is not a substitute for EQ
ReverbBig moments, drop-outs, letting something dissolveOn anything with a busy low end. It turns to fog
RollBuilding into a drop, a stutter on the last beatConstantly. It's the most obviously "look at me" pad on the unit
Hot cuesJumping to the useful part; re-firing a phrase for emphasisNever overused — this is the pad mode to live in

The general rule, and it's the same one the Space & Restraint guide makes about arrangement: an effect only lands if the last few minutes were plain. Contrast requires a baseline. If everything is processed, nothing is a moment.

⑥ The GO+'s Superpower

Stems, and How They Go Wrong

Real-time stem separation — pulling vocals, melody, drums and bass apart from a finished stereo track — is the feature that most distinguishes the GO+ from the original Prime GO. It's genuinely powerful, and it is the fastest way to make a set sound amateurish if used carelessly.

What it's brilliant at

Killing the incoming track's drums so you can blend over the outgoing beat. Dropping an acapella over a different instrumental. Removing a clashing bassline so two tracks can coexist. All of these solve real problems.

What it isn't

Separation is an estimate, not a surgical extraction. Isolated stems have artefacts — smeared cymbals, watery vocals, bass that lost its attack. In a mix, under other music, you won't hear it. Exposed and solo'd on a big system, you will.

Render ahead, not live

Do the rendering in Engine DJ Desktop before the gig — drag a track, a playlist or a crate. Prepared stems load instantly; that's the whole workflow.

Use it to subtract

The best stem move is almost always removing something so two tracks fit, not building a live mashup. Subtraction sounds intentional; addition usually sounds cluttered.

A test worth applying: if a stem trick would still be impressive to someone who didn't know it was a stem trick, it's a good one. If its only value is that you did it live, the crowd cannot tell and it isn't earning its place.

⑦ The Part Nobody Can Teach You Quickly

Reading a Room

Technique gets you a clean mix. What makes someone book you again is where you take the room, and that's an arc — the same shape a song has, stretched over an hour or three. The commonest mistake by a distance is starting too high.

PhaseJobMistake
OpeningSet a tone and let people arrive. Slower, deeper, patientPlaying your best tracks to eleven people. You have nowhere to go for two hours
BuildingRaise energy gradually, in small steps, with occasional pull-backsA straight line up. Constant climb reads as flat
PeakThe tracks people came for. Take the risks hereArriving too early and having to hold it for 90 minutes
AfterCome down deliberately, don't just stopEnding on the biggest track and leaving a cliff

Two things that actually help. Watch the floor, not the screen — you should know within eight bars of a drop whether it landed, and that information only exists if you're looking up. And respect the slot you were given: if you're on before the headliner, your job is to hand over a warm room, not to peak. DJs who understand that get rebooked by promoters more than DJs who play better tracks.

⑧ Useful, Not Sacred

Harmonic Mixing

Engine DJ detects the key of every track, and mixing in compatible keys stops two tracks from clashing harmonically during a long blend. It's a genuinely useful tool and it is routinely over-weighted.

The safe moves are: the same key, one step around the wheel (a fifth away), or the relative major or minor. That's it — that covers nearly everything anyone means by harmonic mixing. The circle of fifths is the same idea musicians have used for centuries; the DJ wheel is a relabelling of it.

It matters most in long blends

Two tracks overlapping for 32 bars with both melodies audible is where a key clash is obvious. On a quick cut, nobody notices or cares.

Percussive tracks are key-free

Drum-led tracks with no strong tonal content mix with anything. A great deal of techno and much drum-focused house is effectively keyless.

Detection gets it wrong

Especially on tracks that change key, or that are ambiguous between relative major and minor. Trust your ears over the label — a clash is immediately audible.

Energy beats key

A perfectly key-matched track that kills the mood is a worse choice than a clashing one that lifts the room. Never let the wheel pick your set.

⑨ The Unglamorous Part

Playing Out

Standalone and battery-powered means you can play anywhere, which also means you'll end up in places with no sound engineer and questionable power. A short checklist prevents almost every disaster.

Before you leaveWhy
Charge fully, bring the PSUSix hours is the rating, not a promise — screen brightness and volume eat it. Plug in whenever mains exists and treat the battery as the backup
Two drives, identicalUSB and SD. If one fails you swap slots and keep playing
Update Engine OS at homeNever on the night, and never over venue Wi-Fi
Carry your own cablesXLR pair, RCA pair, and a 3.5 mm adaptor. Venues lose cables
Know your outputsXLR master to the PA, booth for your own monitoring. Booth level is independent — that's the point of it

Gain staging matters as much here as in a mix. Set channel trims so the meters sit around the top of the green with occasional amber, keep the master out of the red, and let the venue's system make it loud. Running the master hot to compete with the last DJ is how you arrive distorted — the headroom argument is the same one.

If there's a mic input in use, remember it's live. Two combo inputs on this unit means someone will hand you a microphone at some point during the night.

⑩ Getting It Heard

Recording and Releasing Mixes

The GO+ records straight to the USB or SD card, with no computer involved. Record every set, including the practice ones. It costs nothing and it's the single fastest way to improve, because the mistakes you don't notice while playing are extremely obvious on the sofa the next day.

Listen back critically

Note the timestamp of every moment that sags. Almost always it's a phrasing error, a transition held too long, or two tracks that were too similar back to back.

Record with headroom

The recording takes your master output, so a hot master means a clipped file you cannot fix. Leave the same few dB of headroom you'd leave on a mix and raise it afterwards.

Top and tail, lightly

Trim the silence, fade the start and end, and — if it needs it — a gentle limiter to even out level differences between tracks. Don't remaster it. The tracks are already mastered.

Loudness for platforms

SoundCloud and Mixcloud normalise anyway, so crushing your mix buys you nothing and costs you dynamics. Around −14 LUFS integrated is a sane target — see Mastering.

Write a tracklist

It helps platforms identify the music, it credits the artists, and it's the main thing listeners ask for. Engine's history export gives you the running order.

Expect takedowns

Mixcloud licenses mixes and is the safer home; SoundCloud is more likely to flag a copyrighted set. Post to both, and don't be surprised by either outcome.

Fix the beatgrids, cue four points the same way every time, keep everything you need on two drives, count bars before you touch the fader, pull the outgoing bass, use fewer effects than you want to, start lower than feels comfortable, and record every set. The Prime GO+ handles the rest.

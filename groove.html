Groove & Timing

Why perfectly correct music can sound completely dead

You can write the right chords, choose the right sounds, and mix them beautifully, and the track can still feel like nothing. That missing thing is groove — the microscopic timing and loudness relationships that make music feel like it's being played rather than executed. It is not a mystical quality. It's a small number of concrete, learnable decisions about where notes land, how loud each one is, and how long it rings, and every one of them is available to you in a DAW. This page is about how the notes feel; its companion, Rhythm & Beats, is about which notes to play — beat patterns, percussion instruments, technique and practice.

① The Diagnosis

Why 100% Quantized Music Sounds Dead

Quantize snaps every note to the nearest grid line. It fixes sloppy playing, and at full strength it also removes the only information that told you a human was involved. Real players are never exactly on the grid — a drummer's hi-hat lands a handful of milliseconds around the beat, never on it, and those deviations are not errors. They're the groove.

There's a second, less obvious reason perfect timing sounds worse: when several instruments hit at the exact same sample, their transients stack into one hard click and their low frequencies sum coherently, which is both harsh and strangely small. Spread those same hits over a few milliseconds and the attack becomes wider, rounder, and — counter-intuitively — bigger. Part of what you hear as "human feel" is literally a phase and transient phenomenon.

SymptomUsual causeFix
Sounds like a machine100% quantize strength, uniform velocityReduce strength to 70–90%; vary velocity first
Sounds stiff but not roboticEverything dead-centre on the grid, correct but staticPush or pull one element a few ms
Sounds busy and tiringEvery note the same loudness — no accents, no shapeVelocity accents on the strong beats
Sounds thin on the attackLayered hits landing on the same sampleOffset layers by 3–10 ms
Sounds like it's draggingNotes too long, no space between themShorten note lengths; add gaps

Quantize is still the right tool much of the time — a sloppy performance is not automatically a groovy one, and "leaving it human" is not an excuse for a part that's genuinely out of time. The skill is knowing that quantize has a strength parameter, and that it's almost never supposed to be at 100%.

② The Most Underused Control in Music

Velocity Is the Groove

If you change one thing after reading this page, change your velocities. Timing gets all the attention, but loudness variation is what actually makes a part sound played. A run of sixteenth hi-hats at identical velocity is unlistenable; the same pattern with accents on the downbeats and ghost notes in between is a groove — and the notes haven't moved at all.

Velocity usually does more than change volume. On a well-made drum sample it switches to an entirely different recording — a soft snare hit is a different sound, not a quieter one. On a synth it typically opens the filter and shortens the attack. That means velocity variation adds timbral movement too, which is why it reads as expression rather than as level automation.

Accent the pattern

Decide which notes are strong. In 4/4 with straight eighths, the beats are strong and the off-beats are weak — put maybe 20–30 points of velocity between them and the pattern immediately has a pulse.

Ghost notes

Very quiet hits (velocity 20–45) between the loud ones. On a snare this is the entire difference between a beginner beat and a funk drummer. They shouldn't be consciously audible — you should notice their absence, not their presence.

Never repeat exactly

Even accents should vary a few points hit to hit. Two identical bars of a copied loop announce themselves immediately; the same bar with ±5 of drift does not.

Shape across the bar

Let a phrase build slightly toward its landing note and ease off after it. This is what a player does without thinking, and what a grid does not do at all.

In the Piano Roll, Ctrl-⌘-drag vertically on a note to change velocity, or use the Velocity tool. Select several notes and drag to scale them all relatively. For fast humanizing, open Functions → MIDI Transform → Humanize and apply a small random velocity range — but do the deliberate accents by hand first, because random variation on top of a flat part still sounds flat, just noisier.
③ Where the Feel Lives

Ahead of the Beat, Behind the Beat

Musicians talk about playing on top of, in the middle of, or behind the beat, and they mean it literally: a few milliseconds early, dead on, or a few milliseconds late. The tempo doesn't change — the relationship to the click does. This is the single most powerful groove control you have, and in a DAW it's just dragging notes.

PlacementFeelsHeard in
Ahead (−5 to −20 ms)Urgent, driving, anxious, energeticPunk, drum & bass, aggressive rock — often the snare or hats
On the gridTight, modern, machine-preciseMost electronic dance music, the kick almost always
Behind (+10 to +30 ms)Relaxed, heavy, dragging in a good way, soulfulHip-hop, neo-soul, reggae, J Dilla-influenced production

The important part is that different instruments can sit differently at the same time, and that tension between them is the groove. The classic soul feel is a kick locked to the grid with a snare dragging noticeably behind it — the kick keeps the tempo honest while the snare makes it feel heavy and unhurried. Push the bass slightly ahead of a laid-back drummer and the track gains urgency without speeding up. Move the whole kit and you've just changed the timing; move one element against the others and you've created a feel.

Scale matters. At 90 BPM a sixteenth note is about 167 ms, so a 20 ms shift is a subtle lean. At 170 BPM the same 20 ms is a much bigger proportion of the beat and will read as sloppy. Judge these by ear against the tempo, not by the number.

Set the Nudge value in the Toolbar (or Functions → Nudge → Nudge Value) to 1 ms or 10 ms, then move selected notes with Option-←/→. For a whole part, use the track's Delay parameter in the Track inspector — a negative value pulls the entire track ahead of the beat, positive pushes it behind, without touching a single note.
④ The Forgotten Parameter

Note Length & Space

Where a note starts gets all the attention. Where it stops is at least as responsible for how a part feels. The same bassline played with notes ringing into each other is a wash; played staccato with clear gaps, it's a groove you can dance to. Nothing about the pitches or the timing changed.

Short notes create space, and space is what lets a rhythm section breathe — it's the reason funk bass is played with the side of the palm muting the strings, and the reason a great many electronic basslines are gated hard. Long notes create sustain and weight but consume the mix. If a part feels like it's dragging or crowding, try halving every note length before you try anything else.

Gate length as a rhythm control

On a repeated sixteenth pattern, note length is the sound. Long gates blur into a drone; short gates turn the same notes into a percussion part. This is what the synth sequencer's gate control does.

Overlap creates legato

On a monophonic synth, letting one note overlap the next triggers a glide rather than a new attack. Tiny overlaps are how you get a played line instead of a stuttering one.

Release time is note length too

A short MIDI note through a long release still rings. If shortening notes doesn't tighten the part, the envelope is what's holding it open — go there instead.

Vary it like velocity

Identical lengths are as robotic as identical volumes. Let some notes ring a little past their neighbours and clip others short.

Try this on the synth's sequencer: program a sixteenth-note line, then sweep the Gate control from 100% down to 20% while it plays. That single knob takes the same notes from a pad to a pluck to a percussion pattern.

⑤ The Grid, Bent

Swing & Shuffle

Swing delays every other subdivision. Instead of two evenly spaced eighth notes per beat, the first is held longer and the second arrives late — the long-short "ta-tum, ta-tum" that underlies jazz, blues, shuffle, house and most hip-hop. It's usually expressed as a percentage, where 50% is perfectly straight and 66% is a full triplet feel.

AmountFeelTypical of
50% (straight)Even, mechanical, drivingTechno, rock, most pop
54–58%Barely there — you feel it more than hear itModern pop and R&B production, subtle house
58–62%Clearly swung but still tightHip-hop, garage, funk
66% (triplet)Full shuffle, unmistakableBlues, jazz, shuffle rock

Two things beginners get wrong. First, swing usually shouldn't be applied to everything. Swinging the hats while the kick stays straight is a real and very common groove; swinging every single part by the same amount often just sounds like the tempo is wrong. Second, small amounts do the most work. The 54–58% range is where a huge amount of modern production lives, and it's subtle enough that most listeners couldn't tell you it's there — they'd only notice that the straight version feels stiff.

Logic's quantize grid includes swung values directly — 16B through 16F apply increasing swing to sixteenths (and 8B–8F to eighths). The Region inspector's Swing slider adjusts it continuously, and Q-Strength lets you apply the swing only partially. For a specific feel, use Groove Templates: drag a region onto the quantize menu to make its timing a template you can apply to other parts.
⑥ Putting It Together

The Pocket — Kick, Snare, Bass

"The pocket" is the relationship between the kick, the snare and the bass. When those three lock, everything else in the arrangement can be imperfect and the track still feels good. When they don't, no amount of production saves it. Almost all groove work comes down to these three parts.

Kick and bass: lock or interlock

Either the bass hits exactly with the kick — one huge combined event, the default in most electronic music — or it deliberately plays in the gaps between kicks, which is what makes funk and reggae move. What kills a groove is being vaguely near it: a bass note 30 ms off the kick reads as a mistake rather than a choice.

The snare is the backbeat

Beats 2 and 4 are where the listener's body keeps time. This is the element most worth experimenting with — dragging the snare a few milliseconds late is the classic way to make a track feel heavier without changing anything else.

Hats carry the subdivision

They tell you whether the track is in eighths or sixteenths and whether it swings. This is where velocity accents matter most, because the hats are playing more notes than anything else.

Everything else follows

Once those lock, guitars, keys and vocals can sit loosely around them. A rhythm section in the pocket gives everything above it permission to breathe.

A practical starting recipe for a track that feels stiff: keep the kick exactly on the grid, drag the snare +10 ms late, apply 56% swing to the hats only, add velocity accents on the beats with ghost notes between, and pull the bass −5 ms ahead. Then undo each change one at a time and listen to what it was doing. That teardown teaches more than the recipe does.

⑦ Getting Better at This

How to Practise Timing

Groove is an ear skill before it's a technique, and the good news is that it trains quickly because the feedback is immediate — you either feel it or you don't.

ExerciseWhat it builds
Steal a grooveLoad a record you love into the Song Analyzer, get its tempo, then program a beat to that tempo and try to match the feel by ear. You'll discover what you were missing far faster than by reading about it.
Metronome on 2 and 4Play along with the click on the backbeat instead of every beat. Brutal at first, and the fastest way to internalise where the pulse actually is.
One-parameter passesTake a flat programmed loop. Do a velocity-only pass. Then a timing-only pass. Then a length-only pass. Isolating them shows you how much each one is worth.
Play it in badlyRecord a part live with quantize off, however clumsy. Then quantize at 80% strength. You'll keep your feel and lose your mistakes.
Tap alongUse Tap BPM against records and notice how your taps drift. That drift is your timing, and noticing it is the whole skill.

The deepest version of this exercise: take a loop that already sounds good and make it worse on purpose — quantize it to 100%, flatten every velocity to the same value, make every note the same length. Listen to what you destroyed. That's the clearest possible demonstration of what groove is made of.

Groove isn't a feel you're born with — it's velocity, placement and note length, and all three are sitting in front of you in the piano roll. Vary the loudness, move one element against the others, control where notes stop, and stop quantizing to 100%. Then go build one on the synth's sequencer.

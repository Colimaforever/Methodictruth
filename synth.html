📖 Synth Guide

You don't have to wire anything. Click Osc and you have a working synth — an Output appears and the cable is drawn for you. Every module you add after that joins the signal path automatically: sources stack in parallel at the front, effects land at the end just before the Output. Delete something and the chain closes over the gap. Hit a preset up top for a finished patch in one click.

Changing the order

Order is the whole of sound design, so it's a choice, not a consequence of what you plugged in first. Use the ◂ ▸ arrows in a module's header to walk it up and down the chain — filter before delay is a different instrument than delay before filter. ⌗ Tidy re-lays the rack out left-to-right in signal order, so what you see matches what you hear.

Cables are still real, and still yours if you want them: tap an out port and then a glowing in port, or drag from one to the other, for parallel paths and feedback loops the automatic routing won't build on its own. Compatible ports light up and the rest dim. Tap a cable to remove it. Hand-drawn cables are never overwritten by the auto-routing.

Modules

Oscillator
sine = pure/smooth · saw = bright/buzzy · square = hollow/retro · triangle = soft/mellow
Filter
LP cuts highs (warmth) · HP cuts lows (thin/bright) · BP isolates a band · notch scoops one out. Cutoff = where it cuts, Res(onance) = emphasis at the cutoff. Slope = how steeply (−12 gentle → −48 brutal). Drive saturates the signal before the filter, so high Res screams instead of thinning out — the classic acid sound. Push Res near max on LP and it self-oscillates into a playable sine.
LFO
Slow modulation for movement. Pick what it drives from its Drives menu — every parameter in the rack is listed, and choosing one sets a depth range that suits it (Min/Max are the two ends of the sweep, Rate is how fast). Pitch = vibrato · Filter cutoff = wah · Vol = tremolo · Pan = auto-pan. A dashed violet line shows what it's driving. Modulation isn't audio, so no cable can carry it — that's why it's a menu.
FM (Frequency Modulation)
Patch one oscillator's out into another's FM input to modulate its pitch at audio rate. FM Amt = depth in Hz of frequency deviation. Set the modulator at a simple ratio of the carrier: 1:1/2:1 = harmonic (clean, organ-like), 3.5:1+ = inharmonic/metallic (bells, e-piano, gongs).
Envelope (ADSR)
Attack = fade-in time · Decay = drop after peak · Sustain = held level · Release = fade after note off. Patch it between an oscillator and Output and it re-fires on every note — piano keys, MIDI, and the sequencer all retrigger it, so a short Decay gives plucked/percussive steps instead of a flat on/off. Drag the handles on its curve (or the sliders) to shape it — the graph and sliders stay in sync.
Delay
Echo effect. Time = gap between repeats · Feedback = how many repeats · Mix = dry/wet balance.
Reverb
Room simulation. Size = room size · Damping = high-freq absorption · Mix = how much reverb.
Gain
Volume control — use to balance levels between modules.
Noise
white = all freqs equal (hiss) · pink = bass-heavy (wind) · brown = deep rumble.
Audio File
Drop an audio file onto the module (or click Load) to play and loop it — drop in several to build a multi-track mix. Vol sets its level.
Audio In
Live audio from an interface, mic or hardware instrument, processed by everything downstream. Click Enable, allow access, pick your input. Use headphones — see Audio In below.
Pan
Places a sound left/right in the stereo field. −1 = full left, 1 = full right.
EQ
3-band tone shaping. Cut Low to thin out a track, boost High for clarity, pull Mid down to make room for another track ("carving").
Compressor
Evens out volume swings. Thresh = level it kicks in at · Ratio = how hard it squeezes · Atk/Rel = how fast it reacts/lets go.
Chorus
Layers slightly-detuned copies for a thicker, shimmering sound. Rate/Depth = how the detune wobbles.
Limiter
A hard ceiling on loudness — put one right before Output to stop a mix from clipping. Thresh = the ceiling.

Module Categories

Every toolbar button and module card is color-coded by its role in the rack, so a glance tells you what a patch is made of:

  • Source — Oscillator, Noise, Audio File, Audio In: where the sound starts.
  • Shape — Filter, EQ, Envelope: where its tone and contour are carved.
  • Mod — LFO: slow movement driving another module's parameter. Sits outside the audio path; pick its target from the Drives menu.
  • Mix — Gain, Pan, Compressor, Limiter: levels and stereo placement.
  • Space FX — Delay, Reverb, Chorus: depth and width.
  • Out — Output: the final stage, with a live VU meter showing how hot the signal is running.

Mixing Multiple Tracks

Build a mix
Add one Audio File module per stem, route each through its own Pan/EQ into a shared Gain or Compressor, then into one Output. Add a Limiter just before Output to keep the combined level in check.
+ Channel Strip
One click adds Pan → EQ → Compressor → Limiter in mixing order, spliced into whatever you already have, just before the Output. It joins your rack rather than standing up a second one beside it.
VU Meter
Every Output module shows a live level bar under its Master slider — green while there's headroom, amber as it gets hot, red near clipping. Pull the Master fader or add a Limiter upstream if it's pinned red.

Sequencer

Step Sequencer
Open with 🎼 Sequencer in the toolbar. It plays your patched sound — build a basic synth first (Oscillator → … → Output), press Start Audio, then hit Play. Click a step to turn it on or off; drag up or down on it to set its pitch. Set length (8/16/32 steps), BPM, swing, and gate length. The matching piano key lights up as each step fires. Patterns auto-save and reload.
Pitch moves by scale degree, not semitone
Set Key and a scale, and every pitch edit moves within that key — drag once and you land on the next note of the scale, not the next fret. Editing chromatically was the thing that made programming a melody here tedious, because most nudges landed outside the key and you fixed them by nudging again. Pick Chromatic from the scale menu if you want semitones back. Random respects your key too, so it gives you a usable starting point rather than a puzzle.
Velocity — how hard each step hits
Every step has a velocity strip beneath it: drag along it to set how hard that note plays, and the cell fills from the bottom to show it. On the drum rows there's no pitch to set, so dragging up and down on a drum cell sets its velocity directly — which is where ghost notes come from. A pattern where every hit is identical is the clearest giveaway of programmed music; see Groove & Timing for what to do with it. Arrow keys work too: ↑/↓ for pitch, ←/→ for velocity, once a step is focused.
Shaping each step (Decay/ADSR)
The sequencer has no envelope of its own — it plays through whatever's patched on the canvas. Add an ADSR module before Output and set a short Dec (decay) with low Sus (sustain) for plucky, percussive steps, or a longer Decay/higher Sustain for pads that bleed into the next step. It retriggers on every step automatically.
Drum Machine
Below the melodic grid, four drum rows — Kick, Snare, Hat, Clap — on the same clock and step count. Click cells to program a beat; each hit auditions as you click so you can build it by ear while stopped. Beat drops in a four-on-the-floor starting point to edit from — kick on every beat, snare on 2 and 4, and hats on eighths with alternating accents, because a flat hat line is what makes a beat sound typed in rather than played. The drums are synthesized and routed straight to the output, so they work without a patch on the canvas — you can program a beat before you've built a sound. Clear only wipes the melody; Clear Drums only wipes the beat.

Touch Pads (phones & tablets)

Why they exist
Musical typing needs a computer keyboard, and a phone hasn't got one — which left the piano strip, whose keys are narrower than a fingertip. On a touch device the synth opens with pads instead: large multi-touch targets you can actually hit with a thumb. Switch between them any time with ◇ Pads.
Every pad is a right note
The grid is locked to a scale, so there is no wrong pad — pick a root and a scale from the two menus and play freely. It defaults to C Minor Pentatonic, which is the most forgiving scale there is. The amber pads are the root, so you always know where you are.
Chords and runs
Each row starts two scale steps above the row below it, so three pads stacked vertically are a chord — put three fingers down in a column. Sliding a finger across the grid retriggers each pad it crosses, which is how you play a run with one thumb. Hitting the top of a pad plays harder than the bottom.
Range
The −/+ buttons move the whole grid by an octave, same as they do for the piano. Your root and scale are remembered between visits.

Musical Typing

Chromatic rows — the default
The simplest rule a keyboard can have: one long chromatic run, left to right, bottom row upwards. Every key on the four rows plays, every note appears exactly once, and neighbouring keys are neighbouring notes with no exceptions — no skips, no repeats, nothing to remember.
high1 2 3 4 5 6 7 8 9 0 - = q w e r t y u i o p [ ] a s d f g h j k l ; 'lowz x c v b n m , . /
45 keys, 45 semitones — three octaves and a major third, more than double what a two-row layout reaches. The rows join up seamlessly too: / to a, ' to q and ] to 1 are each one semitone, so the whole keyboard is a single ladder. Because no note is bound twice, every key on screen shows exactly one letter.
Piano shape — when you want to see chords
Pick Piano shape for the layout that mirrors an actual keyboard: two manuals an octave apart, letters as the white keys, the staggered row above as the black keys.
black1 2 3 4 5 6 7 8 9 0 - =white q w e r t y u i o p [ ]blacka s d f g h j k l ; 'white z x c v b n m , . /
Chord shapes look the way they look on a piano, which is its whole point — but the cost is that walking along one row skips notes: 5 to 6 jumps F♯ straight to G♯, because the G between them lives on the row below. That is correct as a piano and confusing as a keyboard, which is why it is no longer the default. Its E♯/B♯ slots (a, f, k, ', 1, 4, 8) play the white note they lean against, so nothing here is dead either.
Two rows
Logic Pro's Musical Typing, for anyone with the muscle memory: A W S E D F T G Y H U J up from C, continuing K O L P ; — a tenth in all. Much less range, but it leaves Z/X/C/V free as octave and velocity shortcuts.
Octave and velocity
←/→ shift the whole keyboard down or up an octave across the full MIDI range; ↑/↓ ride velocity. The −/+ buttons beside the octave readout do the same thing with a mouse. On the Two rows layout, Z/X and C/V also work. Holding a note while you change octave is safe — the note you started is the note that stops.
Binding your own keys
Neither layout is fixed. Click ⌨ Remap, click a piano key, then press whatever key you want to play it — repeat for as many as you like, then Esc to finish. The layout menu switches to Custom and your bindings persist between visits, so you can flip out to a preset and back without losing them. Custom is your bindings layered over the current default rather than a frozen copy of it, so a key you never touched keeps following the standard layout and picks up any later improvement to it, while every key you did bind stays exactly as you set it. Right-click ⌨ Remap to reset. A custom binding on a key takes priority over that key's built-in shortcut.

Audio In — external gear through the rack

What it does
The Audio In module takes a live signal from anything your computer can hear and drops it into the patch as a source. An audio interface with a guitar, bass or hardware synth on it; a USB mic; a drum machine; a turntable; a phone on the desk. Everything downstream then treats it exactly like an oscillator — so the Filter, Delay, Reverb, Chorus, Distortion and Compressor all process your instrument, and the LFO can sweep the filter while you play it.
Setting it up
Add Audio In, click Enable, and allow access when the browser asks. The dropdown then lists every input on the machine — pick your interface rather than the built-in mic. Chrome or Edge, and an https:// page (this one is); browsers refuse microphone access over plain HTTP. Nothing is uploaded or recorded: the signal goes to your speakers and nowhere else.
Use headphones
Speakers into a live input is a feedback loop, and it builds to painful in about a second. Headphones remove the problem completely. There's a guard here that cuts the input level if the master output pins for a sustained second, but treat that as a net rather than a plan — it fires after the noise starts.
"It's live but I hear nothing"
Almost always an Envelope in the chain. An envelope is a note gate — it sits shut until a key is pressed — so a continuous signal passing through one is silent between keypresses. The module says so when it detects this and offers a one-click fix. (Deliberately routing an input through an envelope is a real technique: it lets you play a drone or a held pad in and out with the keyboard. It's just never what you want by accident.)
Latency and sound quality
Browser audio adds a few milliseconds on top of your interface's own buffer — fine for effects and for playing along, marginal for tight tracking. Echo cancellation, noise suppression and automatic gain are all switched off deliberately: they're voice-call processing, and on an instrument they gate off reverb tails, eat quiet passages and ride your dynamics away. If your input sounds thin or pumping, it's usually those being re-enabled by the OS rather than by this page — check your interface's own control panel.

MIDI — Controllers and DAWs

Play from a MIDI controller
Plug in a USB MIDI keyboard and allow MIDI access when Chrome prompts you. The MIDI: Connected badge confirms it. CC1 (mod wheel) sweeps filter cutoff; CC74 sweeps resonance, on every Filter module in the patch.
Send notes out to Logic Pro X
The MIDI Out dropdown sends every note this page plays — piano keys, MIDI controller passthrough, and the step sequencer — to an external MIDI port in real time, so Logic Pro X can record or play them through one of its own instruments. Requires three things: (1) Chrome or Edge — Web MIDI isn't supported in Safari or Firefox. (2) A virtual MIDI port — on macOS, open Audio MIDI Setup → Window → Show MIDI Studio, double-click IAC Driver, check Device is online, and make sure it has at least one port (the default "Bus 1" is fine). (3) A Logic Pro track listening on that port — create or select a Software Instrument track, open the track's input in the inspector, and set it to receive from the IAC bus you just enabled (or "All Inputs"). Once that's done, pick the IAC bus from the MIDI Out dropdown here — notes play through your local patch and get sent to Logic at the same time.

Building a Sound

Click these in order and the routing takes care of itself:

  • Basic synth: Osc, Filter, Gain — three clicks, no cables.
  • Add movement: LFO, then set its Drives to Filter · Cutoff.
  • Add space: Delay, Reverb — they land at the end, where space FX belong.
  • Thick sound: a second Osc, detuned a few cents. Sources stack in parallel automatically.
  • Then rearrange: move the Filter after the Delay with ▸ and hear how different the same modules sound.

Sound Design Recipes

  • Warm pad: Saw osc → LP filter (low cutoff, med resonance) → slow LFO on filter → long attack/release
  • Bass: Square osc → LP filter → short attack, med sustain
  • Lead: Saw osc → BP filter (high resonance) → short attack, med release

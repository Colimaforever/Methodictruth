The Mastering Guide

The final polish — loudness, translation, and the last 2 dB, in Logic Pro X

Mastering is the last step — taking a finished mix and making it loud enough, tonally balanced, and consistent so it holds up on every system, from AirPods to a car to a club. It's a game of small moves — a decibel here, a hair of glue there. This guide draws what those moves do and gives you the exact Logic Pro X tools for each.

① The Last 2 dB

What Mastering Is (and Isn't)

Mastering is the bridge between your mix and the world. It does three jobs: raise the track to competitive loudness, apply a final tonal balance so it translates everywhere, and — across an album — make every song feel consistent. What it isn't: a rescue mission. Mastering polishes a good mix a few percent; it can't fix a muddy or unbalanced one. Fix those in the mix and come back.

Unmastered
Dynamic & quiet — lots of headroom, peaks all over. Sounds small next to released tracks.
Well Mastered
Louder & even, but breathing — the peaks and life survive. This is the target.
Over-Limited
Crushed flat chasing loudness — a "sausage." Fatiguing, lifeless, and streaming turns it down anyway.

The whole art is the middle picture. Since every streaming service now normalizes loudness, the loudness war is over — smashing a track flat just costs you punch and gets turned down to the same level as everyone else. Dynamics are a feature, not a bug.

② Set Yourself Up to Win

Before You Master

The best masters start with the right handoff from the mix. Ideally master with fresh ears on a different day — mastering is a decision job, and tired ears make loud, bad decisions.

Leave Headroom

Bounce the mix peaking around −6 dB (no limiter on the mix bus). The master needs room to work — a mix already slammed to 0 leaves nothing to shape.

Fix It in the Mix

If something's wrong — harsh, boomy, a buried vocal — go back and fix the mix. Mastering EQ is broad; it can't surgically undo a mix problem without hurting everything else.

Bounce a Clean File

Export a high-resolution 24-bit WAV at the project sample rate. Master that flat file in a fresh project, not on your busy mix session — you'll judge it more honestly.

Line Up References

Pull 2–3 commercial songs in the same style. They're your target for tone and loudness — gain-match them so you're not fooled by their level.

Raise the Buffer

Mastering plugins are CPU-heavy and you're not recording, so latency doesn't matter — set the I/O Buffer Size to 512 or 1024 for glitch-free playback. It never touches the bounce (that's rendered offline). Full explainer in the Logic buffer & latency guide.

Bounce the mix with File ▸ Bounce ▸ Project or Section (⌘ + B), 24-bit WAV, Normalize: Off. Start a new project, import that file onto a single stereo track, and build your master chain on that track's inserts (or on the Stereo Out). Drag reference tracks onto muted tracks routed straight to Output so your master chain never touches them. Bump the I/O Buffer Size (Settings ▸ Audio) up to 512–1024 so the heavy chain plays back clean.
③ Order of Operations

The Mastering Chain

A master is a short, deliberate chain of gentle processors. Each does a little. The order matters — clean up, glue, shape, color, widen, then raise the level last with the limiter, watching a meter the whole way.

Gentle, in order. Not every track needs every stage — use the fewest that get it there.

Some engineers put a broad subtractive EQ before the compressor (so boomy lows don't pump it) and a tonal EQ after (to shape the glued result) — exactly like a mix chain, just gentler. The limiter is always last: it sets the final ceiling nothing may cross.

④ Shape & Glue

EQ, Compression & Width

On the whole mix, every move is broad and small. You're nudging the balance of the entire song at once, so a 1 dB shelf is a big move and a 3 dB boost is enormous. Reach for wide, gentle curves and low ratios.

Tonal EQ

Gentle, wide moves for the whole-mix balance: a touch of low-end weight, a dip where it's harsh (2–4 kHz), a shelf of "air" up top. Think ±1–2 dB, low Q. A subtle high-to-low tilt fixes most tonal imbalances.

Glue Compression

One gentle bus compressor for cohesion — low ratio (1.5–2:1), slow attack, auto/slow release, only 1–2 dB of gain reduction on peaks. You want it to breathe with the song, not squeeze it. More on how compression works in the Recording Guide.

Multiband — Surgically

When one band misbehaves (boomy lows pumping, harsh highs spiking), a multiband compressor tames just that range without touching the rest. Use it as a scalpel, not a default.

Saturation

A whisper of tape or tube adds density, warmth, and harmonic "loudness" that survives normalization. Drive it gently — on a full mix, a little is a lot.

Stereo Width

Widen the sides for openness with a Mid-Side tool — but keep the bass mono. Wide lows smear and can cancel on club systems & vinyl. Widen the top, anchor the bottom.

Mid-Side EQ

Master-grade trick: EQ the mid (center — vocal, kick, bass) separately from the side (width — cymbals, reverb, guitars). Add air to the sides, clarity to the mid, independently.

Use Linear Phase EQ for tonal moves — it shapes without the phase smearing that matters on a full mix. For glue, the stock Compressor on a low ratio (try the Vintage VCA / SSL bus-style circuit); for problem bands, the Multipressor (Logic's multiband). Width lives in Direction Mixer (Spread, and it does M/S) and Stereo Spread for upper-band width. Match EQ can even "learn" a reference track's spectrum and apply that curve to your master as a starting point.
⑤ Loud, but Right

Loudness & the Limiter

The limiter is a brick wall: it sets a ceiling nothing crosses, then lets you push the whole track into it to raise the average level. Push gently — the goal is competitive loudness with the peaks shaved, not a flattened sausage. Below, the same signal hitting the ceiling: peaks over the line get caught, everything else passes.

The limiter catches the peaks so you can raise everything else — up to a point.

How loud? Loudness is measured in LUFS (perceived loudness) and peaks in dBTP (true peak). Streaming platforms normalize everything to a target, so mastering past it just gets turned down — losing dynamics for nothing. Aim for the target and keep true peak at −1 dBTP to survive lossy encoding.

PlatformNormalizes toNotes
Spotify−14 LUFSLouder masters get turned down to this
Apple Music−16 LUFSSound Check; the quietest target
YouTube−14 LUFSTurns loud masters down, never up
Amazon / Tidal−14 LUFSSame ballpark
Club / DJ / CD−9 to −6 LUFSNo normalization — loudness still matters here
True-peak ceiling−1 dBTPSet this on the limiter, always

A practical target: master to around −14 to −10 LUFS integrated with a −1 dBTP ceiling and it'll translate well everywhere. Don't chase a number at the cost of the music — if 2 dB of loudness costs you all the punch, it's a bad trade. Let the song's energy, not the meter, decide the last dB.

Put the Adaptive Limiter (musical, colored) or Limiter (transparent, with a True Peak mode) last in the chain. Set Output / Ceiling to −1.0 dB, turn on true-peak detection, and raise Gain until you hit your LUFS target — watching the Loudness Meter (below). Keep gain reduction modest; if the meter shows heavy limiting, the mix is too hot going in — turn it down, don't push harder.
⑥ Trust the Meters, Then the Bounce

Metering, Reference & the Bounce

Loudness fools your ears in seconds — meters don't. Master with a LUFS meter open, check your true peak, watch a spectrum against your references, and confirm the stereo image is mono-safe. Then commit with a clean, dithered bounce.

Loudness Meter

Read Integrated LUFS (whole-song loudness — your target number), Short-term (moving), and True Peak (must stay under −1 dBTP).

Spectrum & Reference

A/B your master against commercial references at matched loudness — a real-time analyzer shows if you're too dark, too bright, or bass-heavy versus the target.

Correlation / Mono

Watch the correlation meter and check the whole thing in mono. If the low end vanishes or the image collapses, pull your width back.

Dither on Export

Only when reducing bit depth (e.g. 24-bit → 16-bit for CD), add dither on the final bounce to mask quantization noise. Streaming/24-bit? No dither needed.

Check Everywhere

Bounce it and listen on phone, laptop, car, earbuds. A master that holds up on a phone speaker holds up anywhere — that's the whole point.

Walk Away, Then Decide

Sleep on it. The master that sounded perfect at 1 a.m. often needs a hair less limiting in the morning. Fresh ears are the best mastering tool there is.

Logic's stock Loudness Meter shows Integrated/Short-term LUFS, LRA, and True Peak; the Multimeter adds a spectrum analyzer, goniometer, and correlation meter — put both at the end of the chain (post-limiter). When you're done, Bounce (⌘ + B): for streaming, a 24-bit WAV, Normalize Off; for CD, 16-bit with POW-r dithering. Logic 11's Mastering Assistant can also analyze your track and build a balanced chain with a loudness target in one click — a strong starting point you then adjust by ear.

Mastering is restraint under a microscope — the smallest moves on the whole song, judged against the best in the world at matched loudness. Get the mix right, make a few gentle moves, respect the meters, and stop before you squeeze the life out. That last 2 dB is where a track goes from "done" to released.

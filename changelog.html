What's New, As It Happened

Everything shipped here, newest first. No version numbers — just the work, in the order it happened. RSS feed ⟶

The Song Analyzer now says whether it's working before you paste a link. Its YouTube path runs on a home server behind a tunnel, and when that box was down, or YouTube was blocking it, visitors got "Failed to fetch" and no idea why. The backend now records the outcome of every YouTube download and reports it from /health, and the page shows a status line under the input on load: online and YouTube working, online but YouTube is blocking downloads — drop a file in, or server offline. Network failures mid-session say the same thing in plain words.

Under the hood: a bot-checked download is retried once through YouTube's embedded and mobile players before giving up, since the check is applied per client as much as per address; the JavaScript runtime yt-dlp needs for YouTube's player challenge now prefers Deno, the one yt-dlp actually supports, with Node kept as a fallback; and every link shape works — Shorts, /live/, YouTube Music, youtu.be share links, or a bare video ID.

🌿 September 11, 2026 🌿

The Synth now has sixty presets, in six moods. Thirty-four are new, and every one has a name that says what it feels like and a line that says when you'd want it: Rhodes at Midnight, Warm Blanket, Choir of Ghosts, Sunrise (hold a chord and wait three seconds for the filter to open), Underwater Cathedral, Moog Walk, Neon Bass, Screaming Saw, Flute in the Fog, Theremin, Harp in the Rain, Berlin School, Wind Chimes, Bubbles, Distant Thunder, Broken Radio. The toolbar's preset strip, which had already outgrown a laptop screen, is now six mood buttons — Keys, Pads, Bass, Leads, Plucks & Arps, Texture & FX — each opening a panel of cards, plus 🎲 Surprise me for a sound you didn't choose. Every preset is built from the same modules you can add by hand, so load one and read the rack.

The theory guide's keys are easier to read. A lit key now wears its role across its whole face — amber root, purple third, green fifth, yellow seventh, cyan scale tone — with the note name set in the same colour, instead of a nine-pixel dot you had to lean in for. Keys outside the pattern go dark, so the scale is the bright thing on the keyboard.

And the Synth fits any window. Its navigation bar and toolbar are fixed and both wrap as the window narrows, but the page padded itself for one assumed height, so a custom-sized window put the top row of modules under the toolbar and the bottom row under the keys. The page now measures the nav, the toolbar and the dock and pads itself to fit, live, as the window changes; on a short window the keyboard dock scrolls inside itself rather than swallowing the canvas.

🌿 September 10, 2026 🌿

The Synth has an arpeggiator. Press ⇅ Arp, hold a chord, and it plays one note at a time, in time — up, down, up and down, random, or in the order you pressed the keys, over one to four octaves, at any note length from quarters to thirty-seconds, with a gate for staccato or legato and a Hold that keeps the chord going hands-free. It runs on the same clock as the sequencer, so a programmed beat and a held chord lock together at one BPM. Keys, pads and MIDI all feed it; the sequencer and the theory guide bypass it, since they already know when their notes should land.

And a second envelope. Every subtractive synth since the Minimoog has had one envelope for loudness and one for the filter, and this one only had the first. The new Mod Env module sits outside the audio path like the LFO and points at any parameter from its Drives menu. Aim it at the filter cutoff with a fast decay and every note opens bright and snaps dark, which is what a pluck is; give it a slow attack and a pad blooms; aim it at pitch with a short decay and you have an 808. Oscillators also gained a Coarse tune in semitones — the octave switch on every hardware panel — so a sub-oscillator is −12 and an FM modulator at a 3:1 ratio is +19. And a fix underneath: an LFO or envelope pointed at an oscillator's pitch was only ever wired to the module's silent template, so vibrato did nothing on a played note. Each voice now carries the same wiring.

Eight new presets, and the old Pad rebuilt. Each one is a lesson, and its button says which: Strings and the new Pad are width and slowness (three oscillators, a sub, two envelopes, chorus); Pluck and Trance Pluck are the filter envelope; E-Piano and FM Bell are FM at a harmonic ratio and an inharmonic one; Kalimba is an envelope with no sustain; Chiptune and Arp switch the arpeggiator on with a sound made for it; Reese is wide detune under a driven filter; 808 is the Mod Env driving pitch; Dub Stab is a chord left to a long delay. Load one, change one thing, and listen for what moved.

🌿 September 9, 2026 🌿

The Synth now shows you what to play. Knowing what a scale is and knowing which keys it lives on are different things, and the second one is what you need with your hands on a keyboard. Press ♪ Guide in the piano bar, pick a root and a scale or mode, and every key that belongs lights up while the rest fall back. Play the lit ones and nothing is wrong; the amber key is home. Labels switch between note names and scale degrees, and ▶ Hear it runs the scale through whatever you have patched.

It also knows the chords in a key — the seven the Theory page builds, with their roman numerals — and any chord on any root, from triads to ninths. Tap one and its notes are coloured by their role: amber root, purple third, green fifth, yellow seventh. Three fingers on the coloured keys is the chord, and the purple one is the note that decides major or minor. Every chord chip plays as you tap it, so a progression is something you can hear and then copy.

The Chord Voicings page is rebuilt. It drew each voicing as a single column of numbers, which is a fret list, not a chord chart. Voicings are now real chord boxes — strings across, frets down, a finger number in every dot — and every dot is coloured by what the note is doing, the same five colours as the Synth and the Theory page. A neck map above the boxes shows every place the chord's notes live in twelve frets, and tapping a voicing lights up which of them it uses, so a shape stops being a diagram and becomes a piece of the neck. Every voicing has a ▶ to hear it, because the whole reason to learn a second one is that it sounds different.

The voicings themselves are no longer three templates. Every playable way to hold the chord is searched from the tuning — four fingers, a barre where one finger can lie flat, the played strings sitting together so a strum works — and the page picks a spread up the neck: an open shape, a low one, a middle one, a high one, and a small three- or four-string shape for playing in a band. Filters narrow it to a region. An In the key of row lists the chords that belong together, so you can tap through I – vi – IV – V and see each chord's voicings in turn. And the three pages now hand chords to each other: a chord on the Theory page opens on the Synth's keys or on a guitar neck, and back.

🌿 September 8, 2026 🌿

Seeing the Fretboard's triad section is now diagrams instead of prose. It made a claim about three-note chord shapes and then drew all three of them on one twelve-fret neck, which meant untangling them in your head before you could play any of them. They're now nine chord boxes — the vertical diagram every guitarist already reads — one shape per box, on every string set, with the position marked and each dot labelled by its interval rather than its note name.

And it now says why the shapes work, which is the part that turns nine diagrams into one idea. A triad is three notes and order is the only variable, so every box is the same C, E and G. The inversion picks your melody note, consistently: root position puts the fifth on top, first inversion the root, second inversion the third — on every string set, so "I want the root ringing on top" has an actual answer. And because the third is the only note carrying the chord's quality, moving it down one fret turns any of the nine shapes minor.

The best part is a pair of boxes that settle the B-string question by demonstration rather than explanation. They are the identical three pitches — G, then C, then E — and one is a staircase while the other is a flat barre. The only difference is whether the shape has to cross the G-to-B major third. If you have ever felt a shape "stop working" when you moved it to different strings, that is the whole reason, and it is one fret every time. Every voicing on the page was generated and then read back out of the rendered diagram to confirm the notes are what the text claims.

🌿 September 7, 2026 🌿

The Synth's keyboard has a new default, and a much simpler rule. The piano-shaped layout was faithful to a piano and confusing as a keyboard: because the black keys live on their own row, walking along one row skips notes. Pressing 5 then 6 jumps F♯ straight to G♯, past a G sitting on the row below. Same for 9 to 0. Every row had that problem — not one of the four was a chromatic run.

The default is now Chromatic rows: one long chromatic run, left to right, bottom row upwards. Neighbouring keys are neighbouring notes, with no exceptions — no skips, no repeats, nothing to remember. The rows join seamlessly too (/ to a, ' to q, ] to 1 are each one semitone), so the whole keyboard is a single ladder: 45 keys, 45 semitones, three octaves and a major third. Because no note is bound twice, every key on screen now shows exactly one letter instead of two or three.

The piano-shaped layout hasn't gone anywhere — it's Piano shape in the layout menu, and it's still the one to pick when you want chord shapes to look the way they look on a piano. That's the honest trade: one layout is easier to read as chords, the other is easier to play as a keyboard. Also fixed a cosmetic thing the longer keyboard exposed — the layout ends on G♯, and a black key's negative right margin has nothing after it to pull in, so its box hung nine pixels past the strip and put a scrollbar under a piano that already fitted.

🌿 September 6, 2026 🌿

A bug worth owning. Anyone who had ever rebound a single key on the Synth was silently locked out of every later improvement to the keyboard layout. Remapping stored your map as a frozen snapshot and the loader handed that snapshot back forever — so the day the layout gained new keys, you kept the map exactly as it stood the moment you last touched ⌨ Remap, with nothing on screen to explain why. The piano would even draw the new keys while they made no sound.

Custom is now your bindings layered over the current default instead of a copy of it. Keys you deliberately bound still win — they're applied last — and keys you never touched follow the standard layout, including anything added to it since. Only the difference is stored, not the whole resolved map, because saving the whole thing would bake that day's default into your save and re-create the same trap the next time the layout improved.

🌿 September 5, 2026 🌿

No key on the Synth's keyboard is dead any more. The two-manual layout followed the piano strictly, and a piano has no black key between E and F or between B and C — which left a, f, k, ', 1, 4 and 8 doing nothing at all. Two of those are home-row keys. A dead key under your resting fingers is a worse cost on a computer keyboard than a tidy metaphor is a benefit, so those slots now play the white note they lean against — which isn't a fudge, because E♯ is F and B♯ is C. f plays F. k plays C. Nothing under your hands is silent.

The range grew too. The three keys to the right of P continue the pattern properly — [ and ] carry the white row up to F and G, = is the F♯ between them — taking the layout from two octaves and a third to two octaves and a fifth across 45 keys, with both manuals still exact chromatic runs. Where a piano key now answers to several letters, it shows the main two and lists all of them when you hover it.

Small fix on the Synth: the audio suspended readout sat dead centre at the bottom of the screen, which put it directly over piano keys 12 to 20 — the middle-C region where the two manuals overlap, so precisely the part you play. Nudging it sideways wouldn't have held: the piano is 780px wide on a desktop and 595px on a tablet, so any fixed horizontal offset lands on keys at some width. It now floats above whatever is docked at the bottom, tracking the piano, the touch pads and the sequencer as they open and close. It also stopped intercepting clicks meant for whatever it happens to be floating over — it's a readout, it was never something you click.

🌿 September 4, 2026 🌿

The Synth's sequencer had a bug worth naming. On a phone the melodic grid and the drum grid were sized independently — steps at a fixed minimum width, drums stretched to fit — so they were two different scales sitting on top of each other. Measured at 16 steps on a 390px screen, step 15 of the melody sat 151 pixels away from step 15 of the kick, and the playhead lit two columns that had nothing to do with each other. They now share one scroller and identical column tracks; drift is zero at every width and every pattern length, and a step never shrinks below a tappable size.

Pitch now moves by scale degree. Editing a melody meant clicking −/+ a semitone at a time — seven clicks from C to G, per step, with most stops outside the key. Set Key and a scale and every edit lands in the key: drag up or down on a step and it walks the scale. A note already off-scale snaps onto it rather than staying wrong, Chromatic is still there if you want semitones, and Random now generates in your chosen key instead of always C pentatonic.

And every step has velocity. Previously every note fired at a fixed 100 and every drum hit was a boolean — which is awkward on a site whose own Groove & Timing guide argues that velocity is one of three controls separating music that breathes from music that doesn't. Melodic steps get a velocity strip you drag; drum cells have no pitch to set, so dragging up and down on a drum cell sets its velocity, which is where ghost notes live. The Beat preset now lays hats down with alternating accents rather than flat. Old saved patterns migrate to velocity 100 — exactly what they were already playing at — so nothing you had changes how it sounds.

The grid also became reachable from a keyboard for the first time: 80 cells that were plain <div>s with click handlers now take focus and report their state, with ↑/↓ for pitch and ←/→ for velocity.

🌿 September 3, 2026 🌿

The Synth now takes external gear. A new Audio In module puts a live signal from anything your computer can hear into the patch as a source — an interface with a guitar, bass or hardware synth on it, a USB mic, a drum machine, a turntable. Everything downstream then treats it exactly like an oscillator, so the filter, delay, reverb, chorus, distortion and compressor all process your instrument, and the LFO can sweep the filter while you play it. Click Enable, allow access, pick your input from the list. Nothing is uploaded or recorded — the signal goes to your speakers and nowhere else.

Two things this had to get right. Echo cancellation, noise suppression and auto gain are all switched off — they're voice-call processing, and on an instrument they gate off reverb tails, eat quiet passages and ride your dynamics away, which is most of why browser audio has a reputation for sounding bad. And feedback: speakers into a live input builds to painful in about a second, so an input that pins the master output for a sustained second has its level cut automatically. Use headphones anyway — the guard is a net, not a plan, because it fires after the noise starts.

The interesting failure was silence. An Envelope is a note gate: it sits shut until you press a key, so a continuous signal routed through one makes no sound at all, and the default patch has one. Every path was correct and the meter read nothing — which is exactly what a broken feature looks like. Rather than quietly routing around it (the way round the envelope also skips the filter, and running gear through the filter is most of the point), the module detects the gate, says so plainly, and offers a one-click fix. Deliberately putting an input through an envelope is a real technique — it lets you play a drone in and out from the keyboard — it's just never what you wanted by accident.

The Synth's computer keyboard now uses the whole keyboard. It followed Logic Pro before — A W S E D F T G Y H U J and a few more — which is seventeen keys and a tenth of range, leaving two entire rows doing nothing. The default is now the tracker layout: your keyboard read as two pianos stacked an octave apart, one per pair of rows. The Z row is white keys with S D G H J as the black keys above them; the Q row is the same shape an octave up with the number row on top. Because the rows are physically staggered, each black key sits where it would sit on a real piano.

That's 34 keys across two octaves and a third — double the range, and enough that both hands can play at once without ever touching the octave control. Octave and velocity moved to the arrow keys so Z X C V are free to be notes. If you had the old layout in your fingers, a Two rows option in the new layout menu restores it exactly, and anything you bound by hand with ⌨ Remap is kept as Custom — so you can flip between a preset and your own map without losing either.

🌿 August 21, 2026 🌿

New tool: the Harmony Workbench. Most chord tools hand you symbols. This one shows you the voices — because a progression doesn't sound classical because of which chords it uses, but because of how the inner lines move between them. Build a progression and it draws all four parts (S/A/T/B), colouring each connection by whether that voice held, stepped or leapt. Load the descending lament and you can see the whole thing move in parallel steps, which is the entire reason it sounds the way it does.

The suggestions aren't a lookup table — each candidate chord is actually voice-led and scored on the motion that results, so when it recommends ♭VImaj7 after i and says "3 voices hold," it has computed that Am and Fmaj7 share three notes. The palette is stocked for the darkwave and classically-inflected vocabulary: suspensions, added ninths, minor-major sevenths, the Neapolitan, and the borrowed ♭III/♭VI/♭VII. It spells enharmonically by function too — the third of E major shows as G♯, not A♭, because it's a leading tone.

Two guides alongside it. Writing Harmony explains the moves — why holding notes matters more than choosing chords, what a suspension is actually delaying, the borrowed chords of natural minor, the descending lament bass and the chromatic inner line, pedal points, and what Pieter Nooten specifically does in Clan of Xymox (natural minor with the leading tone absent, added ninths as a default, inner voices carrying the tune, and very few chords held for a very long time).

And The 4AD Sound — how a record label became an adjective. Ivo Watts-Russell's curation as the creative act, Blackwing Studios and John Fryer, and the defining production idea: reverb as an instrument rather than a finishing touch — printed, committed, timed to the tempo, and often louder than the source feeding it. Plus guitar used as texture rather than riff, the voice mixed as one colour among many, and Vaughan Oliver's sleeves as part of the same statement. It only works on sparse arrangements, which is the same argument the restraint guide makes.

The Synth now opens on a sound you can play. An empty rack meant the first thing a visitor did was press a key and hear nothing. It now starts on a Prophet-style patch — two sawtooth oscillators detuned seven cents against each other into a resonant lowpass, shaped by an envelope and widened with a slow chorus. That detuning is where most of the warmth in a classic analogue poly comes from, and it's why that instrument sounds the way it does. Presets are still one click away, and a shared patch link still overrides it.

Fixed: module cards were cut off on phones. Slider value readouts were being pushed clean off the right edge of the card, so you couldn't see what any control was set to. The cause was a flexbox trap — a range input has an intrinsic width of about 129px, and flex: 1 won't shrink a flex item below its automatic minimum width, so the slider held the row open at 220px inside a 132px card. Everything now fits, with the sliders keeping usable travel.

Also refreshed the site's page titles and descriptions to match what's actually here now — the production hub still described itself as a "three-part guide" when it's grown to ten, and the synth's description predated the touch pads, the drum machine and the auto-routing. They're now written around the problems people actually search for rather than as feature lists.

New: DJing on the Prime GO+. First DJ guide on the site, written around the Denon DJ Prime GO+ — a two-deck standalone system with stem separation and a six-hour battery that needs no laptop at all. Half of it is getting the hardware out of your way: preparing a library in Engine DJ, why beatgrids are the single highest-value prep task (a wrong grid drifts mid-mix and there's nothing you can do live), cueing the same four points on every track so your hands stop reading the screen, and rendering stems ahead of time rather than during a set.

The other half is craft that transfers to any gear. Sync solves the easy problem — phrasing is the hard one, and no machine does it for you: dance music is built in fours, and a mix starting on the wrong bar feels wrong even when the beats line up perfectly. Also: pulling the outgoing bass so only one track owns the low end, why a set of nothing but long blends has no punctuation, using fewer effects than you want to, the energy arc and why starting too high is the commonest mistake there is, harmonic mixing as useful-not-sacred, a pre-gig checklist, and recording every set — including practice ones, since the mistakes you miss while playing are painfully obvious the next day.

On stems specifically, the honest version: separation is an estimate, not surgery. It's brilliant for killing an incoming track's drums so you can blend over the outgoing beat, or removing a clashing bassline so two records coexist — and the best stem move is almost always removing something so two tracks fit, not building a live mashup. Subtraction sounds intentional; addition usually sounds cluttered.

New: Seeing the Fretboard. The Fretboard Explorer could always show you notes and intervals across tunings, but it never explained how to read any of it. This does. The premise: most guitarists hit a wall they can't name — they can play what they've memorised and nothing else — and the wall is that the fretboard gets taught as a set of positions to remember when it's really a map of relationships. A 22-fret guitar has 138 note positions for 44 distinct pitches, so memorising positions means memorising the same information three times over.

The most useful section is octave shapes, which most players are never taught at all: from any note, the same note an octave up is two strings and two frets away — or three frets, once the B string is involved. Learn those and you only ever have to memorise two strings, because the shapes give you every other note for free. That's an afternoon's work, not a year's. Also in there: why the B string is the only irregularity on the instrument and how it explains every shape that "stops working" higher up, position as a tone choice rather than a lookup, escaping the pentatonic box by playing along a string instead of across, triads on three-string sets (how professionals actually comp), targeting chord tones instead of running scales, and what an alternate tuning really changes. Every fretboard diagram is generated from the tuning itself, so the notes on them can't be wrong.

The Synth now has a real instrument on phones. Musical typing needs a computer keyboard, and a phone hasn't got one — which left the piano strip, whose keys are narrower than a fingertip. Open the synth on a phone or tablet now and you get touch pads instead: a grid of large multi-touch targets, locked to a scale so there is no wrong pad. Pick a root and a scale (it starts on C minor pentatonic, the most forgiving there is), and the amber pads mark the root so you always know where you are.

The layout does something a single row of keys can't: each row starts two scale steps above the one below it, so three pads stacked vertically are a chord. Put three fingers down in a column and you get one. Slide a finger across the grid and it retriggers every pad it crosses, which is how you play a run with one thumb — and hitting the top of a pad plays harder than the bottom, so there are dynamics on a surface that otherwise has none. Root and scale are remembered between visits, and ◇ Pads switches back to the piano whenever you want it.

New: Space & Restraint. Everyone repeats less is more and almost nobody can act on it, because it's advice about a feeling rather than a method. This is the method — starting with why sparse music actually hits harder, which turns out to be three facts about hearing rather than a matter of taste: people can consciously track only three or four simultaneous streams, masking is physical (two sounds in the same range literally reduce each other's audibility inside your ear, which is not something EQ can undo), and contrast needs a baseline, so if every section is maximal then none of them is. The practical consequence is sharp: a part you can't clearly hear isn't making the track bigger, it's making everything else smaller.

Then silence as something you play rather than the default state music fills — the full stop before a chorus, the drop-out, reggae's missing downbeat, the unresolved phrase. Where restraint comes from, since it was arrived at independently several times: minimalism proper, ambient, modal jazz, techno, neo-soul, and dub, which gets its own section as the clearest case study in music — engineers making versions by taking things away live at the desk, which is where the breakdown, the delay throw and the filter sweep all come from. The transferable habit: perform your mutes instead of drawing them.

Eight concrete techniques for doing less — the mute test (mute each track for eight bars; if nothing gets worse, delete it, don't mute it), capping the track count, one idea at a time, stripping the verse down instead of building the chorus up, the reverse build. And an honest counterweight the slogan usually omits: when minimalism fails. Empty and spacious are different things, and the difference is entirely in what remains — the fewer elements you have, the better each one must be. A dense arrangement can carry a mediocre snare; a four-element track gives the listener nothing else to look at. That's why restraint is expensive rather than easy.

New: Rhythm & Beats. Melody gets written about endlessly and rhythm gets treated as the thing underneath it, which is backwards for most of the music anyone actually listens to. This is the note-level half — which hits go where, and why. What makes a beat good (you can find the one; three bars of repetition and a fourth that breaks it; space; two-bar question-and-answer; only one layer is busy), and the diagnostic that catches most failures: can you sing it? Real rhythms are memorable the way melodies are. The most common beginner mistake isn't simplicity — it's uniform density.

The heart of it is ten load-bearing beat patterns drawn as sixteen-box grids — the backbeat, four-on-the-floor, boom bap, trap, the funk beat with its ghost notes, reggae's one drop, tresillo, dembow, bossa nova and afrobeat. They're written in exactly the layout the synth's drum machine uses, deliberately, so you can read one here and type it in there in twenty seconds. Notice how few notes most of them contain: the ones that feel most complex are complex in their accents, not their note count.

Then the instruments, framed as jobs rather than objects — a beat is three roles, not three drums. Something marks the pulse, something marks the backbeat, something keeps the subdivision; fill them with a stomp, a clap and a shaker and you still have a beat. Ten percussion instruments and what each one actually adds (why a real shaker part has an alternating accent, the three conga tones, why a stacked clap sounds human and a single one sounds thin), the techniques that make a part sound played — ghost notes, rimshot versus cross-stick, flams, buzz rolls, heel-toe — and the four rudiments actually worth your time. If you program rather than play, the rudiments still matter: they tell you what's physically possible, and a fill nobody could stick sounds synthetic even when every note is in the right place.

It also goes somewhere most rhythm writing stops short of: clave, tresillo and polyrhythm. Clave isn't a drum part, it's a rule — an asymmetric two-bar reference the whole arrangement agrees with, and an idea that's portable to any genre. Tresillo (3-3-2) is probably the most widespread rhythmic figure in popular music worldwide and the shortest path to making a four-square beat lean. And the cheapest trick on the page: put a three-note cycle on a percussion layer over a 4/4 beat, and it returns to the downbeat every three bars instead of every one — four bars of an otherwise static loop suddenly have a shape. Closes with nine rhythm exercises, none of which need an instrument.

The Music Education menu is now grouped rather than being one list of fifteen links — The Path, Craft, Reference, Trainers. Same links, four short lists instead of a wall.

New: The Mixing Guide — the missing part of the path. The production series went Record → Arrange → Effects → Master, with Effects standing in for mixing. But knowing what a compressor does isn't the same skill as mixing, and it showed: the word panning did not appear anywhere on this site. Mixing now has its own guide, and it starts where a mix actually starts — the static mix, built with nothing but faders and the pan pot before a single plugin opens. Then gain staging and headroom, panning and the stereo field, the low end (who owns the sub, keeping the bottom mono, when to high-pass and when not to), subtraction as the alternative to EQ, and automation as the honest alternative to over-compression.

That guide closes with the thing underneath every other skill: how to listen. Your room, your speakers, your volume and your fatigue are all part of the signal chain, and unlike your plugins they lie to you constantly. Why untreated rooms make you bake in the opposite of the error you're hearing, what headphones are good and bad at, why you should pick one monitoring level and keep it, and how to use a reference track properly — level-matched, because a mastered record is far louder than your mix and louder always wins an A/B for reasons that have nothing to do with mixing. The same rule applies to your own plugins, and it explains a great deal of what people believe processing does for them.

New: Groove & Timing. You can write the right chords, choose the right sounds and mix them beautifully, and the track can still feel like nothing. Groove isn't a mystical quality — it's velocity, note placement and note length, three controls already sitting in your piano roll. Why 100% quantize sounds dead (and why layered hits landing on the same sample sound smaller), velocity accents and ghost notes, playing ahead of and behind the beat and what each feels like, note length as a rhythm control, how much swing is actually used in modern records, and how kick, snare and bass lock into a pocket.

New: Choosing Sounds, on the least glamorous and highest-leverage idea in production — the right sound needs almost no processing, and the wrong one can't be processed into it. Always audition with the track playing rather than in solo, because what matters isn't how a snare sounds but what it sounds like in the space left over by everything else. What you're actually judging (frequency footprint, transient, length, character, tuning, width), how to layer so each part contributes one thing, why a layered kick sometimes gets thinner and how flipping polarity fixes it, and how to stop a 4,000-sample library from turning selection into procrastination.

New: Finishing — the skill nobody teaches. Almost everyone has a folder of unfinished eight-bar loops, and not for lack of knowledge. Six reasons tracks stall and the different remedy each one needs, how to escape loop purgatory (copy the loop across three minutes first — a bad complete song can be improved anywhere, a perfect sixteen bars can only be extended), what to do when you're stuck, a workable definition of done, and how to ask for feedback that isn't "yeah, it's cool." Listeners are excellent detectors and poor diagnosticians; take the problem, ignore the prescription.

The Arrangement guide gained a section on register — arranging in the vertical. Everything else on that page is about what happens when; this is about what frequency range each part occupies and whether two of them are in the same place at the same time. It's the real cause of most muddy mixes, and no EQ can separate two parts genuinely playing the same notes in the same octave. Masking is an arrangement problem, and it's far cheaper to solve there.

The Synth now wires itself. Click Osc and you have a working instrument — an Output appears and the cable is drawn for you. Everything you add after that joins the signal path on its own: sources stack in parallel at the front, effects land at the end where they belong. Delete a module and the chain closes over the gap instead of going silent. A modular synth is a wonderful thing to understand and a miserable thing to be handed cold, and dragging four cables before you hear a note was the wrong first five minutes.

Because the order is now the interesting decision rather than the tedious one, every module in the chain got ◂ ▸ arrows to move itself along the signal path. Filter before delay is a different instrument than delay before filter, and that comparison is now two clicks instead of a re-patch. ⌗ Tidy lays the rack out left-to-right in signal order so the picture matches the sound. Drag a module anywhere you like and the auto-layout backs off for good.

The LFO can finally be pointed at things. Modulation isn't audio, so no cable could ever carry it — which meant until now the only way to get an LFO onto a filter cutoff was to load a preset that already had one. It now has a Drives menu listing every parameter in the rack, in signal order, and picking one sets a depth range that actually suits it: choose Filter · Cutoff and the sweep becomes 200–4000 Hz instead of the ±100 that would have done nothing. A dashed violet line shows what's driving what. Vibrato, wah, tremolo and auto-pan are each one menu pick.

Cables are still real and still yours — tap or drag to build parallel paths and feedback loops the automatic routing won't invent — and a cable you drew by hand is never re-routed by the automatic wiring.

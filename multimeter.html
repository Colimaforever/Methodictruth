Reading the Meter

A Multimeter for Instrument Repair

A multimeter turns an invisible problem into a number. The amp hums, the pedal is silent, the guitar cuts out when you move — and none of that tells you where. The meter does. It is the single tool that converts "it's broken" into "it's broken here," and almost everything else in repair follows from that one conversion.

This is written around a Fluke 115, but the symbols are standard. Learn them on this meter and you can pick up any meter in any shop and know what the dial is offering you.

The Three Quantities

Voltage · Current · Resistance

Electricity moving through a circuit is described by three numbers, and they are locked together. Understand what each one is and the dial stops being a mystery, because every position on it is just a different way of asking one of these three questions.

Voltage — the push

Voltage is potential difference: how hard the electricity is being pushed from one point to another. Measured in volts (V). The word difference is the important part — voltage is never a property of one point, it is always measured between two. Asking "what's the voltage here?" is incomplete in the same way "how far is it?" is incomplete. Between here and what?

In practice one of those two points is nearly always ground — the chassis, the sleeve of the jack, the black lead's home. A 9 V pedal supply means nine volts of push between the barrel's tip and the sleeve.

Current — the flow

Current is rate of flow: how much charge is actually moving past a point each second. Measured in amperes (A), or for anything in a pedal, in milliamps (mA) — thousandths of an amp.

Voltage can exist with nothing flowing at all. A 9 V battery sitting in a drawer has the full push and zero current, because there is no path. Current only exists when there's a complete loop. This is why current is the awkward one to measure, and the reason is worth holding onto: to measure flow, you have to become part of the loop.

Resistance — the restriction

Resistance is opposition to flow, measured in ohms (Ω). A guitar cable has almost none. A pickup has several thousand ohms of fine wire. A broken solder joint has effectively infinite resistance — which is exactly what "broken" means electrically.

Ohm's Law — the relationship

V = I × R  ·  I = V ÷ R  ·  R = V ÷ I

Push equals flow times restriction. Know any two and the third is arithmetic. This is the whole of basic circuit theory, and it is genuinely enough to diagnose most faults in a passive instrument or a simple pedal.

The water analogy, and where it breaks

Voltage is water pressure, current is litres per second, resistance is how narrow the pipe is. It's a good analogy and worth using. Two places it misleads, though: water leaks out of a broken pipe, whereas current simply stops if the loop is broken — nothing pours onto the floor. And pressure at a dead end is still pressure: a live wire touching nothing has full voltage and no current, and it is still perfectly capable of hurting you the moment you complete the loop.

The One Rule That Matters

Across, through, and never on a live circuit

More meters are killed by this than by anything else, and it is a single idea: each quantity requires a different kind of connection.

Voltage is measured across a part, with the circuit live — the meter sits alongside and barely disturbs anything. Current is measured through — you break the circuit and insert the meter into the gap. Resistance is measured with the power off and at least one leg of the part lifted.
  • Voltage — in parallel, circuit live. Touch the probes either side of whatever you're interested in. The meter has enormous internal resistance on this setting, so almost no current detours through it and the circuit carries on as if you weren't there.
  • Current — in series, circuit live. You must break the circuit and put the meter in the gap, so all the flow goes through it. On this setting the meter is nearly a plain piece of wire — almost zero resistance.
  • Resistance and continuity — power off, always. The meter supplies its own small test current. Any voltage still present in the circuit fights that and gives a meaningless reading, and can damage the meter. Lift one leg of the component too, or you'll be reading everything else in parallel with it.
The expensive mistake

Because the meter is near-zero resistance in current mode, putting the leads across a voltage source while still set to amps creates a dead short through the meter. On a 9 V supply you'll blow the fuse. On mains you'll get a flash, a bang, and possibly an injury.

The habit that prevents it: when you finish a current measurement, move the red lead back to the volts jack immediately — before you put the meter down. The physical position of the lead becomes the safety interlock, so you can never absent-mindedly probe a voltage in amps mode.

The Dial

Every symbol, and what it's asking

Two marks do most of the work. A straight line (or a line over dots) means DC — direct current, the steady one-direction supply from a battery or a pedal adapter. A wave means AC — alternating current, the back-and-forth of mains power, and of audio signal itself. Put either next to a V and you have "DC volts" or "AC volts." That one rule decodes most of the dial.

The buttons

  • Yellow (Function). Cycles the shared positions — on the ohms detent it steps between resistance, continuity, diode and capacitance. If the meter seems to be on the right setting but reading nonsense, this is usually why.
  • HOLD. Freezes the reading. Genuinely useful when both hands and both eyes are committed to holding probes somewhere awkward inside a chassis.
  • MIN/MAX. Records extremes over time. This is how you catch an intermittent — the fault that behaves perfectly while you're looking at it.
  • RANGE. The 115 autoranges by default, which is almost always what you want. Manual range is for when a reading hovers between two ranges and keeps flickering.
Reading the display

Watch the unit prefix, because autoranging changes it without warning. m = thousandths, k = thousands, M = millions. A pickup reading 8.02 kΩ is eight thousand ohms; the same display showing 8.02 Ω would mean a catastrophically shorted coil. OL is not an error — it means over limit, the resistance is beyond what the meter can read. On continuity, OL simply means open: no path. That's often exactly the answer you wanted.

The Jacks

Three holes, one of which bites

The black lead lives in COM and never moves. The red lead lives in VΩ for everything except current. Moving the red lead into the A jack is a deliberate act, and it should feel like one.

That jack connects through a very low resistance shunt, protected by a fuse — on the 115, an 11 A, 1000 V fast-blow (Fluke part 803293). The fuse is the only thing standing between a mis-set meter and a genuinely dangerous fault current, which is why replacing it with a hardware-store fuse of the same amp rating is a bad idea: the interrupt rating, not the amp rating, is what does the protecting.

One honest limitation worth knowing before it confuses you: the 115 has no separate milliamp jack. Everything goes through the 10 A range, and with a 6000-count display that lands you at roughly 1 mA of resolution. A pedal drawing 8 mA reads as 0.008 A — technically correct, practically one significant figure. If you need real precision on small currents, measure the voltage across a known 1 Ω resistor in the supply line instead and let Ohm's law do the rest; 10 mV across 1 Ω = 10 mA.

What To Actually Measure

The measurements that find real faults

Cables — start here, always

Set continuity. Probe tip to tip: it should beep. Sleeve to sleeve: beep. Tip to sleeve: silence — if that beeps, the cable is shorted and your signal is going to ground. Then the part most people skip: leave the probes on and flex the cable at both plugs while you listen. An intermittent that only appears under movement is the single most common fault in a working musician's rig, and it is invisible to a static test.

Pickups

Ohms, across the pickup's two leads with it disconnected from the pots. A humbucker typically reads somewhere around 7–16 kΩ, a single coil around 5–8 kΩ. The absolute number matters less than what it isn't: OL means a broken coil — the winding has gone open somewhere and the pickup is dead. A reading near zero means it's shorted. Either way you've answered the question in about four seconds.

Worth knowing this is DC resistance, not impedance, and it is not a measure of output or of tone — it's a coarse indicator of how much wire is on the bobbin, and a reliable test of whether that wire is intact.

Pots and switches

Ohms across the outer two lugs gives the pot's value — a 250 k pot reads near 250 kΩ and shouldn't change as you turn it. Now put one probe on an outer lug and one on the wiper (middle) and sweep the control slowly. The reading should climb smoothly. If it jumps, drops out, or flickers, that's a scratchy pot shown as a number — dirt or wear on the track. For switches, continuity between the common and each position tells you immediately whether the switch is actually switching.

Pedals and power

  • Check the supply first. DC volts, probe into the barrel: centre pin to sleeve. Most pedals want centre-negative, so expect to read −9 V — the minus sign is information, not an error. A supply reading 9 V on the wrong polarity will kill a pedal that lacks protection.
  • Test batteries under load. A 9 V battery out of the drawer reads 9 V and tells you nothing. Measure it while the pedal is on. A tired battery holds its voltage until asked for current, then sags — under load, anything below about 7.5 V is finished.
  • Measure draw before you build a board. Amps, in series with the supply line. Add up what your pedals actually take rather than what the enclosures claim, then buy a supply with headroom. Digital delays and reverbs draw far more than analogue boxes and are what usually overrun a supply.
  • Trace a dead pedal by voltage. With power on, check you have supply voltage at the board, then at the IC's power pin. Voltage present at the jack but absent at the chip means the fault is between them — a broken trace, a dry joint, a failed protection diode.

Instrument wiring

Continuity from the output jack sleeve to the back of every pot, to the bridge, and to the control cavity shielding. All of it should be one connected ground. A hum that changes when you touch the strings, and a bridge that doesn't beep to the jack sleeve, is a diagnosis rather than a mystery.

Staying Alive Around Amplifiers

Read this before opening anything that plugs into a wall
Tube amps can kill you after they are unplugged

A valve amp runs plate voltages of 300–500 V DC and upward, and its filter capacitors store that charge for a long time after the amp is switched off and unplugged. There is no hum, no glow, and no warning. People have been killed by amplifiers that had been sitting unplugged for days.

Solid-state amps, powered speakers and anything with a mains transformer deserve the same caution. The danger is not the loudness of the thing, it's the wall.

If you are learning, learn on passive instruments, cables and 9 V pedals. There is a great deal to master there and none of it can stop your heart. Move to mains-powered gear deliberately, with proper guidance — one hand behind your back, never working alone, and only after you know how to discharge filter caps safely and have verified with your own meter that they are at zero.

  • Respect the CAT rating. The 115's CAT III 600 V is about how much transient energy the meter can survive, not just steady voltage. It is fine for everything in a studio or on a stage.
  • Inspect the leads. Cracked insulation near the probe tip is the most common way a safe measurement turns into a shock. Check them before you trust them.
  • Connect black first, remove red first. A habit borrowed from high-voltage work, and free to adopt.
  • Assume it is live until your own meter says otherwise. Not the switch, not the plug being on the bench, not somebody's word. Measure it.

Finding The Fault

Bisection — the method behind the measurements

Knowing what the dial does isn't the same as knowing where to point it. The method that works is the same one used to find a bug in code: cut the problem in half, test, discard the half that's fine, repeat. A signal path from guitar to speaker might have fifteen stages; bisection finds the broken one in about four measurements instead of fifteen.

  1. Describe the fault precisely. Dead silent, or weak? Always, or only when something moves? On every setting, or one? "Intermittent when the cable is wiggled" and "silent always" lead to completely different hunts.
  2. Halve the path. Don't start at the guitar. Start in the middle — plug straight into the amp, bypassing the board. Working or not, you've just eliminated half the rig.
  3. Substitute what's cheap to substitute. A known-good cable takes ten seconds and settles the most likely cause outright.
  4. Now reach for the meter, on the half you've narrowed to. Continuity for paths, DC volts for supplies, ohms for components.
  5. Compare against a known-good example. A meter gives numbers, not verdicts. The fastest way to know whether 6.1 kΩ is right is to measure the working pickup next to it. Where you can, write down the readings from your gear while it's healthy — a pedal's supply voltage, each pickup's resistance. Diagnosis stops being guesswork the moment you have a baseline.
The habit worth building

Measure things that aren't broken. Take readings across your working rig now, while everything is fine, and keep them somewhere. It costs an afternoon, teaches you what the meter's numbers feel like when nothing is wrong, and turns every future repair from an investigation into a comparison.
